### Where the pilot finds no effect, and how to interpret the difference

1.  **LTE rollout at the metro and county levels.** Most of the natural experiments cited in the *Anxious Generation* literature estimate the effect of *first* wired broadband access in the 2000s, or of Facebook first arriving on college campuses. By contrast, 4G LTE in 2011–2013 was a speed upgrade layered onto existing 3G smartphones and mobile Facebook; most large U.S. metros received LTE within a two-year window; and if social media operates partly through peer norms and national platforms, teenagers in later-adopting markets were exposed alongside early-adopting ones. Each of these factors attenuates geographic comparisons across U.S. markets. Finding a null effect for LTE timing therefore indicates that U.S. metro rollout dates cannot isolate the 2010s shift, not that smartphones were harmless.  
      
    The closest geographic parallel that does find an effect in the smartphone era is Elaine Guo's [British Columbia study](http://hdl.handle.net/1807/130332). Guo compares teenagers in rural mountain communities that gained high-speed fixed wireless internet (25 Mbps over cellular infrastructure) with teenagers in mountain communities that remained unserved, comparing girls with boys before and after 2014 using administrative school records on severe mental-health designations (which require a clinical diagnosis). Starting in 2014, girls in covered communities experienced a roughly 1 percentage point increase in severe designations relative to boys—about 90% of their baseline rate—with no corresponding shift for boys, moderate designations, or unrelated disabilities. Three features distinguish Guo's setting from our U.S. LTE panel: the treatment is a transition from little or no home broadband to high-speed access in isolated towns rather than a 3G-to-4G upgrade in metropolitan areas; the outcome is a clinical school designation rather than a census disability screener; and the time contrast compares pre- and post-2014 periods against a 2019 coverage map rather than staggered rollout dates (which is also a limitation of that study, since earlier annual coverage maps were unavailable). Taken together, the two analyses suggest that geographic designs can detect a 2010s connectivity effect only where broadband access itself was newly introduced and where the outcome captures clinical internalizing disorders.
2.  **Country-level GSHS panels with national internet, mobile-broadband, and 3G/4G series.** Regressing country-round survey means on national ITU penetration series is subject to the same aggregation problems that Haidt and coauthors [highlight](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21677026261425910) in Vuorre and Przybylski's 72-country Facebook study: national averages pool adult and youth access and move with broader economic trends. Our country-level GSHS regressions should be read with the same caution.
3.  **Caregiver reports in 67 MICS surveys.** The MICS analysis provides a more substantive null result. Across 507,478 children in low- and middle-income countries, caregiver-reported anxiety and depression among 10–17-year-olds show no meaningful relationship with local young-adult internet use or with a teenage girl's own daily phone or internet use within the same sampling cluster; the small positive cluster correlation for girls' anxiety is mirrored among 5–9-year-olds who do not use phones. Two interpretations remain open: either the self-report correlations in school surveys partly reflect reverse causation and shared reporting style (distressed adolescents both use phones more and report more symptoms), or caregivers frequently miss internalizing symptoms in their children (consistent with clinical evidence that parent–child agreement on internalizing disorders is low). Public cross-sections cannot separate these two explanations, which motivates linking adolescent self-reports to external data on local network rollout.
