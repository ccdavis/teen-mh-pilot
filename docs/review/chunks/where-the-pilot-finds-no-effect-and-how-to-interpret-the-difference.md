### Where the pilot finds no effect, and how to interpret the difference

1.  **LTE rollout at the metro and county levels.** Most of the natural experiments cited in the *Anxious Generation* literature estimate the effect of *first* high-speed broadband access ([Donati et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3949645); [Arenas-Arroyo et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhealeco.2025.103014)), of Facebook first arriving on college campuses, or of early U.S. smartphone exclusivity. On that last point, [Myers and Hooper (2026)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35310) show that during AT&T's exclusive U.S. carrier monopoly on the iPhone from June 2007 to February 2011, pre-existing county-level AT&T 3G coverage strongly predicted early iPhone adoption, crowding out teens' in-person socializing and reducing 15–19 birth rates by 4.5–8.0%. By the time commercial 4G LTE rolled out across U.S. metros in 2011–2013—and after Verizon began carrying the iPhone in February 2011—3G smartphones and mobile social media were already spreading nationwide, and most large metros received LTE within a narrow two-year window. Finding a null effect for 4G LTE launch dates therefore shows that 2011–13 metro speed upgrades are too late and compressed to isolate the U.S. smartphone transition, whereas pre-2011 AT&T 3G coverage and county broadband availability offer sharper variation.  
      
    The closest post-2010 geographic parallel that does find a mental-health effect is Elaine Guo's [British Columbia study](http://hdl.handle.net/1807/130332) (alongside [Arenas-Arroyo et al.'s](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhealeco.2025.103014) Spanish fiber-to-the-home study using hospital records). Guo compares teenagers in rural mountain communities that gained high-speed fixed wireless internet (25 Mbps over cellular infrastructure) with teenagers in mountain communities that remained unserved, comparing girls with boys before and after 2014 using administrative school records on severe mental-health designations (which require a clinical diagnosis). Starting in 2014, girls in covered communities experienced a roughly 1 percentage point increase in severe designations relative to boys—about 90% of their baseline rate—with no corresponding shift for boys, moderate designations, or unrelated disabilities. Three features distinguish Guo's setting from our U.S. LTE panel: the treatment is a transition from little or no home broadband to high-speed access in isolated towns rather than a 3G-to-4G upgrade in metropolitan areas; the outcome is a clinical school designation rather than a census disability screener; and the time contrast compares pre- and post-2014 periods against a 2019 coverage map rather than staggered rollout dates (which is also a limitation of that study, since earlier annual coverage maps were unavailable). Taken together, these studies suggest that geographic designs detect a 2010s connectivity effect when they capture initial high-speed access or pre-2011 carrier exclusivity and measure clinical or self-reported internalizing outcomes.
2.  **Country-level GSHS panels with national internet, mobile-broadband, and 3G/4G series.** Regressing country-round survey means on national ITU penetration series is subject to the same aggregation problems that Haidt and coauthors [highlight](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21677026261425910) in Vuorre and Przybylski's 72-country Facebook study: national averages pool adult and youth access and move with broader economic trends. Our country-level GSHS regressions should be read with the same caution.
3.  **Caregiver reports in 67 MICS surveys.** The MICS analysis provides a more substantive null result. Across 507,478 children in low- and middle-income countries, caregiver-reported anxiety and depression among 10–17-year-olds show no meaningful relationship with local young-adult internet use or with a teenage girl's own daily phone or internet use within the same sampling cluster; the small positive cluster correlation for girls' anxiety is mirrored among 5–9-year-olds who do not use phones. Two interpretations remain open: either the self-report correlations in school surveys partly reflect reverse causation and shared reporting style (distressed adolescents both use phones more and report more symptoms), or caregivers frequently miss internalizing symptoms in their children (consistent with clinical evidence that parent–child agreement on internalizing disorders is low). Public cross-sections in MICS alone cannot separate these two explanations, but they can be tested directly in the U.S. by linking parent and teen reports in NHIS-Teen and internationally by expanding the DHS-8 self-report mental-health panel.
