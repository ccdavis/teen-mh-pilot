### Two empirical patterns the existing debate has largely overlooked

- Perceived schoolwork pressure rose after 2010 across roughly 45 HBSC countries, not only in the United States, and psychosomatic complaints rose more in countries where pressure increased more. Although the within-country slope is modest and matches the pre-2010 baseline relationship, the post-2010 rise in perceived academic pressure is a broad cross-national trend that dismissals based on single-country exam reforms do not address.
- In U.S. state youth surveys with post-2021 waves, adolescent sadness and suicidal ideation have fallen substantially from their 2021 peaks—in Washington, 2025 sadness is below its 2010 rate—without a corresponding drop in daily smartphone or social-media use. Neither a simple screen-dose account nor a simple academic-pressure account explains that downturn without additional mechanisms, such as changes in school phone restrictions or shifts in platform content.

**Summary.** Prior literature documents a consistent individual-level correlation between heavy screen use and adolescent distress alongside causal effects from earlier broadband and Facebook rollouts, while Guo's British Columbia study extends that causal evidence to rural wireless access and clinical diagnoses after 2014. Our pilot reproduces the individual-level association in developing-country school surveys, but finds that U.S. 4G rollout dates, county adoption rates, and caregiver reports in 67 MICS surveys show no corresponding effect. Resolving whether the self-reported association is causal requires pairing adolescent-reported symptoms and phone use with exogenous local variation—most promisingly, the staggered 2024–26 U.S. school phone bans and subnational mobile-coverage rollouts.
