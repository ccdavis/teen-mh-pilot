### Two empirical patterns the existing debate has largely overlooked

- Perceived schoolwork pressure rose after 2010 across roughly 45 HBSC countries, not only in the United States, and psychosomatic complaints rose more in countries where pressure increased more. Although the within-country slope is modest and matches the pre-2010 baseline relationship, the post-2010 rise in perceived academic pressure is a broad cross-national trend that dismissals based on single-country exam reforms do not address. Combined with [Hansen, Sabia, and Schaller's (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.1222-12710R2) finding that U.S. youth suicides drop sharply every summer, fell by 18–25% during the spring 2020 pandemic school closures despite surging screen time, and rebounded when in-person schooling resumed, the evidence points toward an interaction (H3): digital peer comparison and notifications are most harmful when layered onto the social and academic stressors of the school year.
- In U.S. state youth surveys with post-2021 waves, adolescent sadness and suicidal ideation have fallen substantially from their 2021 peaks—in Washington, 2025 sadness is below its 2010 rate—without a corresponding drop in daily smartphone or social-media use. Neither a simple screen-dose account nor a simple academic-pressure account explains that downturn without additional mechanisms, such as the re-tightening of school phone restrictions or shifts in platform content.

**Summary.** Prior literature documents a consistent individual-level correlation between heavy screen use and adolescent distress alongside causal effects from initial broadband rollouts ([Donati et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3949645); [Arenas-Arroyo et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhealeco.2025.103014); [Churchill and Johnson 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34614); [Guo 2023](http://hdl.handle.net/1807/130332)), early iPhone carrier exclusivity ([Myers and Hooper 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35310)), and the school calendar ([Hansen, Sabia, and Schaller 2024](https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.1222-12710R2)). Our pilot reproduces the individual-level association in developing-country school surveys, but finds that 2011–13 U.S. 4G launch dates, county smartphone-adoption rates, and caregiver reports in 67 MICS surveys show no corresponding effect. Resolving where the causal effect lies requires pairing self-reported and clinical outcomes with sharper local variation—including the 2024–26 U.S. school phone bans, pre-2011 carrier coverage, school-year seasonality, and linked parent–teen surveys.
