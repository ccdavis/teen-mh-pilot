Teen Distress Pilot

Two books offer rival explanations for the rise in adolescent anxiety and depression after 2010: Jonathan Haidt's The Anxious Generation (smartphones and social media) and Peter Gray's account of intensified schooling. This report asks what large-scale public microdata can say about them today, before any licensed or restricted data are requested, and whether that gets us closer to an answer than reading either book. Every figure is computed from IPUMS microdata (ACS, ATUS, MICS, IPUMS International) and public files; every chart has hover values.

The four hypotheses

The study was designed around four candidate explanations, and every test below is meant to separate at least two of them.

Part 1 · The rise, and whether public data can measure it

Before either hypothesis can be tested, the outcome has to be established: is there a rise, is it real rather than a change in reporting, and which public measures track it. This part uses the American Community Survey, the national depression and sadness surveys, and the state youth surveys.

The ACS cognitive-difficulty item, 2008–2024

The American Community Survey asks, of everyone 5 and older, whether the person has "serious difficulty concentrating, remembering, or making decisions" because of a physical, mental, or emotional condition. The question has been stable since 2008 (2005–07 used different wording and is excluded). Each year samples about 240,000 12–17-year-olds. Standard errors use the 80 successive-difference replicate weights.

Does the ACS item measure adolescent distress?

Three checks: (a) does the ACS item move with a real depression measure at the state level, (b) is its rise concentrated in girls, (c) does its national timing match the survey series. The public NHANES depression screener covers ages 18+ only (the adolescent file is restricted), so NHANES enters as an 18–24 check.

National timing

All series in percent, with 95% CIs. NSDUH points sit at the midpoint of each two-year period; the 2021+ NSDUH estimates use a new web mode and DSM-5 wording and are not comparable to earlier years. YRBS sad/hopeless was first asked in 1999.

NSDUH youth depression was flat at about 8% from 2005 through 2010–11 and then rose almost every period to 15% in 2018–19. YRBS sadness was flat from 1999 to 2013, then rose sharply in 2017–2019. The ACS item rises gradually from 2012 and accelerates after 2019. The ACS series turns up at roughly the right time but its amplitude is a fraction of NSDUH's: about +13% relative versus +85%.

Do states move together? (check a)

51 states. Each point is the state's change in both measures between 2008–10 and 2017–19; dashed line is the OLS fit (slope 0.37, SE 0.18, r = 0.22).

Across states there is no relation between the levels of the two measures (the ACS item is a disability screener, NSDUH is a diagnostic interview), but their changes are related: a state-and-period fixed-effects regression of NSDUH youth MDE on the ACS rate gives a slope of 0.14 (SE 0.05, p = 0.01) across 714 state-by-period observations, and the decade-long changes correlate at 0.22 (slope 0.37, SE 0.18). Against the YRBS state files (39 states, by sex), the ACS rate moves together with "sad or hopeless" at a slope of 0.35 (SE 0.18) with state-by-sex and year-by-sex effects, and not at all with "considered suicide."

Conclusion. The ACS item passes the sex check and moves, weakly but genuinely, with depression within states. It can serve as one main outcome, with two caveats: it captures perhaps a sixth of the depression rise, and the part of its change that is specific to girls is small relative to sampling error. The state youth surveys (the YRBS state files here, and the county surveys later in the report) deserve equal standing rather than a supporting role.

Geographic variation: is there anything to explain?

For a rollout design to work, the change among 12–17-year-olds has to differ across places by more than sampling noise. Using metropolitan areas (plus each state's non-metro remainder) as the unit, the average change from 2008–10 to 2017–19 is about half a percentage point, the true spread across metros is about the same size, and the sampling error for a typical metro is a bit larger still. In other words, real differences between places exist, but no single metro's change can be trusted on its own; the metros have to be analysed together. (Statistics: weighted mean +0.47 pp, between-metro SD 0.46 pp, median sampling error 0.64 pp, Cochran's Q 218 on 116 df, I² ≈ 0.47.)

State youth surveys at county level

The state youth surveys are the self-report outcome the ACS lacks. County tables were compiled for six states: Minnesota (1998–2019, 87 counties), Washington (2010–2025, 38), Maine (2009–2023, 16), California (2013–2019, 54, by sex), Pennsylvania (2015–2023, 60) and Illinois (2018–2024, 78), 34,439 county rows with source and wording recorded for each. Minnesota's pre-2013 sadness item has different wording and is excluded from the sadness series.

Unweighted county means over the grades each state reports in every survey year (Washington 8, 10 and 12; Maine's and Pennsylvania's all-grade rows; Minnesota grade 9; California 7, 9 and 11; Illinois 8, 10 and 12). Minnesota's 2022 county tables sit behind a CAPTCHA and are not yet included.

Three things stand out. Sadness rose in every state from about 2013 to a 2021 peak (Washington 28% → 41%, Maine 22% → 37%, Illinois 35% → 41%). Minnesota's suicidal-ideation series (grade 9), the only one that reaches back to the 1990s, fell from 24% in 1998 to 12% in 2013 before turning up. And the post-2021 declines are large: Washington's 2025 sadness rate (27%) is below its 2010 level and Illinois' 2024 ideation rate (13.5%) is the lowest in its series.

Part 2 · Phones and social media (H1)

The phone hypothesis predicts that distress rose first where and when smartphones and mobile data arrived, that heavier users are worse off, and that the same pattern appears in countries that went straight to phones. The tests run from the coarsest exposure (when a metro got 4G) to the finest (an adolescent's own reported hours).

4G rollout across U.S. metros

The central test of the phone hypothesis: if fast mobile data mattered, teenagers in metros that got 4G first should have turned worse first.

LTE launch dates were compiled from carrier press releases and their press reprints: 967 carrier-market launches (Verizon 442, AT&T 243, Sprint 234, T-Mobile 47), mapped to 2013 CBSA codes, with a first-launch date for 98 of the top 100 metros and for 279 of the 311 metros the ACS identifies. Verizon's December 2010 launch covered 36 of these metros; 125 more launched in 2011, 96 in 2012, 14 in 2013. Onset is assigned to the school year that starts after the launch. Coverage gaps (mainly Verizon's 2013 small-market waves and about 44 AT&T dates among the top 150) are listed in the methods note.

Descriptive: onset cohorts

Population-weighted ACS cognitive-difficulty rate for 12–17-year-olds in metros grouped by first LTE launch, indexed to each cohort's 2008–10 mean.

What this shows. LTE timing across U.S. metros does not predict the timing of the rise in the ACS item. It does mean the rollout design cannot get anywhere with the ACS alone: it needs the state youth surveys or clinical outcomes at the metro level.

Phone-first countries: MICS Child Functioning Module (68 surveys, 2017–2024)

MICS6 and MICS7 (2017 onward) carry the Washington Group / UNICEF Child Functioning Module for every child aged 5–17: the caregiver reports how often the child "seems very anxious, nervous or worried" and how often "very sad or depressed" on a daily / weekly / monthly / a few times a year / never scale, with identical wording in every country. The same surveys ask women 15–49 (and men in about half the surveys) about mobile ownership and how often they used the internet and a mobile phone in the past three months, and the household questionnaire records internet access. All 68 samples on the IPUMS-MICS server with the module were read from the fixed-width files: 507,478 children with valid responses in 67 surveys.

One point per survey, ages 10–17. Cross-survey variation in caregiver-reported anxiety is enormous (from under 1% in Turkmenistan to 40% in Tunisia) and is dominated by reporting culture, which is why all tests below are within country.

Girls aged 10–17 in clusters where more young adults use the internet daily are reported slightly more anxious (+0.34 pp per 10-point rise in the cluster share, SE 0.13). But a similar coefficient appears for 5–9-year-old girls (+0.19, SE 0.13), who are not the exposed group, so this is a property of connected clusters rather than of adolescent phone use. Mobile ownership in the cluster is negatively related to anxiety for every age and sex, household internet is small and positive for girls only (+0.26 per 10 points, SE 0.12, with nothing at 5–9), and depression shows no relation to any measure of connectivity. The within-cluster own-use comparison, the closest MICS comes to the individual exposure Haidt describes, shows no effect or negative for girls: daily internet use −0.1 pp on anxiety (SE 1.4) and −1.8 pp on depression (SE 1.3), daily phone use −2.2 pp (SE 1.2). For boys the own-phone-use coefficients are positive (+4.4 and +5.5 pp, SE ~2) but rest on the quarter of boys with a men's-questionnaire record. The learning and friendship functioning items are flat throughout.

DHS-8 self-reports: Nepal 2022

The DHS-8 mental-health module gives a self-reported counterpart to the MICS caregiver items. Nepal 2022 administered the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 to a subsample of 7,442 women aged 15–49, alongside internet-use frequency and smartphone ownership; Kenya 2022 asked only about diagnosed depression or anxiety. Among Nepali women aged 15–19 (n = 1,379; 4.5% with PHQ-9 ≥ 10, 42% using the internet almost daily), daily internet users are 2.3 pp more likely to screen positive for depression (SE 2.4) and 3.5 pp for anxiety (SE 2.9) than non-users in the same sampling cluster, with wealth, education and age held fixed; the same coefficients are positive at 20–24 and negative at 25–49. Smartphone ownership itself is unrelated. In Kenya, diagnosed depression among 15–19-year-olds is unrelated to owning a smartphone (−0.1 pp, SE 1.3). These are single-survey cross-sections and too small to be decisive, but they line up with the GSHS hours gradient rather than with the MICS caregiver no effect, which is itself a finding: the association appears when the adolescent reports her own state and her own use.

Countries with both a MICS6 and a MICS7 round on the server (Pakistan excluded: its rounds are different provinces). Turkmenistan's rise is from a very low base; Kyrgyzstan and Tunisia fall; Mongolia is mixed.

What this shows. This is the out-of-sample test the study design asked for, and it does not find the smartphone effect: neither cluster-level connectivity nor a girl's own daily internet or phone use predicts caregiver-reported anxiety or depression once the country and region are held fixed, and the one positive cluster coefficient is matched by the 5–9-year-olds (a placebo comparison, since they are not the exposed group). Qualifications: the outcome is a caregiver report, not self-report; exposure is measured as use rather than as the timing of network coverage, so the design with GSMA coverage maps remains the one that could establish cause; and the before-and-after comparisons will sharpen as more MICS7 rounds are released.

Phone-first countries: census microdata (IPUMS International)

Kenya 2009/2019 and Mexico 2010/2020 were scanned as the best pairs. Both 2020-round censuses carry the Washington Group item on difficulty remembering or concentrating, and Kenya 2019 also asks each person about mobile phone ownership and internet use. But the 2010-round items were yes/no "mental disability" questions, so the two-point change is dominated by wording. Within 2019/2020, prevalence at 12–17 is 0.8–1.8% (any difficulty) and 0.2–0.7% (a lot or cannot), boys exceed girls at every child age, and district-level regressions of the 12–17 rate on teen phone ownership or internet use (Kenya, 76 district-sex observations) or household internet (Mexico, 3,259 municipality-sex observations) give coefficients within ±0.01 pp per percentage point of exposure level. The census item behaves like a developmental-disability measure and is not sensitive to adolescent distress. This evidence can only corroborate, not test.

Part 3 · School pressure (H2)

The school-pressure hypothesis predicts that distress rose where and when schooling became more demanding: in the high-stakes years, in the school year, in states that adopted accountability testing, and in countries where students report more schoolwork pressure. The tests run from time use, to the cross-national HBSC series, to U.S. state policy.

Did school demands rise? Time use of 15–17-year-olds (ATUS, 2003–2024)

The American Time Use Survey gives a direct look at the school-demand channel. On school-year weekdays, homework time rose from about 50 minutes (2003–08) to about 70 minutes (2015–19) and stayed there; sleep did not fall; in-person socializing drifted down before collapsing after 2020; television fell and games rose. Note the small samples (roughly 200–800 teens per pooled period) and that phone use is mostly recorded as a secondary activity, which ATUS does not capture.

Part 4 · The interaction (H3): phones in the same hours as high-pressure school

The interaction hypothesis in its sharpest form says that what harms adolescents is not phones as such but phones during school hours, on top of high-stakes academic demands in the same hours: social comparison and notifications running through the day that also carries the tests and the grades. If that is right, three things follow. Pressure and phone use should both have risen after 2010 (they did, in most HBSC countries and in U.S. homework time). Removing phones from the school day should bring relief out of proportion to the hours removed, and more relief for students under more academic pressure. And out-of-school use, or use in low-pressure settings, should matter less.

School phone bans

School phone policy is the one policy both theses agree should matter if phones are the mechanism, and it is the observable from the four-hypothesis table that the first pass had not touched. This section sets what public data allow as of September 2026 beside the causal literature.

Read as a picture, the two panels move in opposite directions twice. Prohibitions fell from 91% to 66% between 2009–10 and 2015–16 while depression and sadness rose from their 2011–13 lows; prohibitions then climbed back through 2019–20 and, after the pandemic peak, sadness fell in every state series as the 2024–25 wave of state mandates arrived. The correlation is far from clean: the sadness series kept rising through 2019–2021 while prohibitions were already recovering, and the post-2021 fall began before most state laws took effect. Two hypotheses fit the shape, and neither is established:

  • Partial re-restriction plus awareness. The modest recovery in school restrictions from 2016, together with rising attention from parents, schools and adolescents themselves, may have reduced the most acute in-school and late-night exposure even before formal bans. The PISA cross-section (less in-school use, less device anxiety, less loneliness among girls in ban schools) and Norway's staggered bans are consistent with this, but both are small effects and the U.S. timing is loose.
  • Content rather than access. If what mattered in 2012–2017 was image-based social comparison (Instagram, Snapchat), the later migration of teen attention toward algorithmic consumption feeds (TikTok, YouTube) could raise total screen time while producing fewer acute internalizing symptoms. This would explain rising hours alongside falling sadness, and it is the mechanism the Facebook-rollout study points to (social comparison), but the pilot has no platform-mix data by year and cannot test it.

Lags and enforcement

Two features of the policy make the simple overlay above too demanding a test, and both argue for reading the chart with a lag of a year or two.

  • Delayed effects are what the causal studies show. In the 2026 U.S. pouch study, student well-being fell by 0.2 SD in the adoption year and only turned positive (+0.16 SD) by the second year; Norway's bullying and mental-health effects grew over three years; the Florida district study found suspensions up 12% in year one, fading in year two. The pattern is resistance first, then adjustment, with administrators refining enforcement over a school year or more. Under that pattern the 2015–16 low point in restrictions would be expected to show up in outcomes around 2017–18, and the 2024–25 mandates around 2026–27, which is roughly what the overlay shows and is also why the 2025 YRBS is only the first, weakest post-ban wave. The U.S. test should therefore follow states for at least two years after a ban, not compare a single before and after.
  • "Policy" is not enforcement. PISA 2022 measures both. Among schools that report phones are not allowed on the premises, a median 46% of students across 52 systems still used a smartphone at school daily (62% in non-ban schools), and in 24 of the 52 a majority did. The United States is among the weakest: 70% daily use in ban schools against 82% elsewhere, a gap of 12 points, compared with gaps of 30–46 points in Japan, Taiwan and Brunei. Enforcement in the U.S. in 2022 was therefore mostly nominal, which is consistent with the idea that restrictions only began to take hold as administrators gained confidence, and with the SSOCS series measuring stated policy rather than practice. It also means any effect found in the 2025–27 waves will be a lower bound on what full enforcement could do.

Country-by-country daily-use rates in ban and non-ban schools are in results/tables/pisa_ban_enforcement_by_country.csv.

The 2025 YRBS, the 2025–27 state-survey waves, and the district-level rollouts already in the policy table are what will turn the shape into a test: bans arriving at different times in different states and districts, against outcomes measured before and after, by sex, with the 65+ placebo comparison retained.

In detail: schools relaxed phone prohibitions exactly when distress rose: from 91% prohibiting in 2009–10 to 66% in 2015–16, during the years when smartphones replaced feature phones. Prohibitions then climbed back (77% by 2019–20) and the 2024–25 wave of state laws has pushed bell-to-bell policies toward a majority of schools. That is a national series, so it cannot separate school policy from everything else that changed; but it means the 2010s rise happened while access to phones in school was expanding, which fits either thesis, and the 2023–25 fall in sadness in the state surveys coincides with the re-tightening.

Two readings. On mental health specifically, the strongest evidence is Norway's staggered middle-school bans (fewer psychological consultations for girls, less bullying, no effect for boys), which is the girl-specific pattern H1 predicts. The English one-time comparison shows no effect on well-being, and the 2026 U.S. Yondr-pouch study finds well-being dips at adoption and recovers by year two with no change in online bullying. Achievement effects are small and positive where found, and vanish in Sweden. So bans are a plausible policy with mixed evidence rather than a settled one, and the mental-health effect, where it appears, is on girls.

Three findings. First, bans change behaviour, partly: daily smartphone use at school is 12 points lower in ban schools (from a 55% base, so 43% of students in ban schools still use one daily), leisure screen use at school falls, and after-school use is unchanged, which is the right placebo comparison pattern. Second, the well-being differences run in the direction the Norwegian study found and are concentrated in girls: less nervousness without a device, 1.4 pp less loneliness at school for girls (none for boys), and +0.02 to +0.03 SD on belonging and life satisfaction. Third, students in ban schools report less classroom distraction by digital devices (3.5 pp fewer say they are distracted in most or every lesson; 4.2 pp for girls, 2.9 for boys), in line with the lower in-school use. All of this is a comparison across schools at one point in time: schools choose whether to ban, and nothing here says how a ban school would have looked without its ban, so the small favorable girl-specific differences could reflect which schools ban as much as what bans do.

What this shows. The only public, multi-country microdata on school phone rules show a modest, girl-specific association between bans and lower loneliness and device anxiety, alongside clear evidence that bans reduce in-school use and reported classroom distraction. Because the comparison is across schools at one time, it cannot separate the effect of a ban from the kind of school that adopts one. Together with Norway's result, that is enough to make the 2024–25 U.S. state bans the most valuable natural experiment now pending in this project; the 2025 YRBS and the 2025–27 state-survey waves are where it will be tested, using the approach set out above.

How this sits with Haidt's and Twenge's evidence

The Anxious Generation research pages and the collaborative reviews present a large body of evidence for a link between social media, smartphones and adolescent distress, much of it from Jean Twenge's work with the Monitoring the Future, YRBS and Millennium Cohort surveys (the model-curve analyses, the "heavy users twice as likely" comparisons, the worldwide loneliness series) and from the natural experiments the book relies on. This section sets that evidence beside the pilot's results. The short version: the pilot reproduced the individual-level association that literature rests on, in new settings; what it did not find is any population-level timing effect, and the studies that establish causation on those pages are not tests of the 2012 smartphone shock either.

Where the pilot agrees

  • GSHS 2022–23, five countries: girls on a phone 3+ hours a day report loneliness +8 pp and suicidal ideation +10 pp, rising with hours. That is an odds ratio of roughly 1.5 to 1.7, close to the magnitude the reviews call "twice as likely".
  • Nepal 2022 self-reported PHQ-9 and GAD-7 point the same way for 15–19-year-olds.
  • The U.S. timing facts match: NSDUH turns up in 2011–12, YRBS sadness later, girls dominate in every source, the ACS 65+ placebo comparison falls.
  • The NTIA smartphone exposure level predicts YRBS girls' sadness within states (suggestive; it did not replicate at county level).

Where the pilot shows no effect, and why that is not a contradiction

  1. LTE onset, at market and county level. The natural experiments on the Anxious Generation pages identify off first wired-broadband access in the 2000s, or Facebook arriving where there was none. LTE in 2011–2013 was a speed increment on top of 3G smartphones and mobile Facebook that already existed; most large metros got it within 24 months of each other; and, as Haidt himself argues, the harm is a network effect, so a teen in a late-LTE market was exposed through peers and national platforms anyway. Each of those pushes a market-level estimate toward zero. The absence of an LTE effect says the 2010s shock is not identified by U.S. geography, which is different from saying there was no shock. None of the studies on those pages tests the 2012 smartphone transition with a rollout design either; the causal leg of the case is about earlier technologies.
  2. Country-level GSHS with internet penetration, mobile-broadband subscriptions and 3G/4G coverage. These are the same kind of evidence as Vuorre and Przybylski's 72-country Facebook study, which Haidt's team criticizes for aggregating groups and technologies. That criticism applies to the pilot's country-level no-effect results too; they should not be leaned on.
  3. MICS, 68 surveys. The one genuinely new negative result. Caregiver-reported anxiety and depression for 10–17-year-olds show no relation to cluster connectivity or to a girl's own daily internet or phone use, and the one positive cluster coefficient is matched by the 5–9-year-old placebo comparison. Two readings are both live: the self-report association is partly reverse causation and shared method (distressed teens use phones more and report both), or caregivers do not see internalizing symptoms (parent–child agreement on internalizing problems is known to be low). The pilot cannot separate these, which is exactly why the GSMA coverage-timing design on the adolescent-reported rounds is the next step.

Two things the pilot adds that those pages do not address

  • Schoolwork pressure rose across roughly 45 HBSC countries after 2010, not only in the U.S., and complaints rose more where pressure rose more. It behaves as a stable correlate rather than the cause of the break, but Gray's premise is not a U.S. peculiarity, and the review's dismissal of school pressure rests on one UK exam study.
  • The U.S. state surveys show a large reversal after 2021 (Washington's 2025 sadness rate is below its 2010 level) with no comparable fall in phone use. A pure exposure level story has to explain that; so does a school-pressure story.
In sum. The Anxious Generation evidence establishes a consistent individual association and a plausible causal mechanism from earlier internet rollouts. The pilot confirms the association, including in phone-first countries, and shows that the population-level version of the claim is not recoverable from the geography of 4G or adoption in public data, and does not appear in caregiver-reported outcomes. Someone convinced by Haidt should conclude, a little more than before, "the effect, if causal, is not visible in rollout timing and needs adolescent-reported exposure to detect"; a skeptic should conclude "the association replicates everywhere self-report is used." The decisive test remains the one neither side has run: adolescent-reported exposure against local coverage timing.

Are we closer to an answer?

Closer than the books, in three specific ways, and not close on the question the books actually argue about.

HypothesisWhere it stands after the pilot
H1 · Phones and social mediaThe individual association is consistent and reappears in places the book's evidence does not reach: girls who use phones heavily report more loneliness and suicidal thoughts in Ghana, India, Jamaica, Morocco and Nepal as in the United States, with the same sex pattern and exposure level gradient. But every test that uses timing or adult-measured exposure shows no effect: 4G rollout across U.S. metros and counties, adoption exposure level at county level, cluster connectivity in 68 MICS surveys, coverage in 39 GSHS countries. Either the effect is real but travels through peers and platforms so evenly that geography cannot see it, or the association is largely reverse causation and shared causes. The public data cannot yet distinguish these; school phone bans (PISA, Norway) lean modestly toward a real effect for girls.
H2 · School pressureGray's premise holds up better than Haidt's collaborative reviews allow: schoolwork pressure rose after 2010 in most HBSC countries, not only the U.S., and U.S. homework time rose about 40%. But pressure behaves as a stable correlate of complaints rather than the cause of the break, U.S. accountability shocks show no effect, and the age pattern lacks the high-stakes-years signature. The real limitation is measurement: "pressure" is one survey item, while phone use can at least be counted in hours, so H2 is harder to test than H1 rather than refuted.
H3 · InteractionNot supported through state policy crossed with 4G timing, but the sharper version, phones during school hours on top of high-stakes academics, fits the ban evidence best: bans cut in-school use, girls in ban schools report less loneliness and device anxiety, the association is larger for students under high schoolwork anxiety (not significantly), Norway's bans reduced girls' psychological consultations, and U.S. sadness moved inversely to school prohibitions. It is the hypothesis the pending U.S. ban studies and the HBSC microdata can test directly, and the one that would reconcile the two books.
H0 · MeasurementRejected as the main story: the rise is present in self-report, caregiver-report and diagnostic measures, the 65+ placebo comparison moves the other way, and it is concentrated in girls everywhere. Reporting and diagnosis-seeking plausibly add to it (the rise is steepest among affluent teens), but do not create it.

Three findings that neither book contains. First, the rise was broad and nearly simultaneous across places with very different connectivity and schooling histories, which is why coarse designs cannot separate the theses and why both camps can find support in national trends. Second, the post-2021 reversal in the U.S. state surveys is as large as the 2013–2021 rise, without a comparable fall in phone use, and it coincides with schools re-tightening phone rules; a pure exposure level story and a pure school-pressure story both have to explain it. Third, the association between phone use and distress appears only when the adolescent reports both her own use and her own state; caregiver-reported symptoms in 68 countries show nothing. That is the fact the next phase has to resolve.

What would settle it, and what this project should do next.
  • Follow the 2024–26 U.S. state and district phone bans through the 2025–27 YRBS and state-survey waves, by sex, for at least two years after each ban, with the 65+ comparison group retained. The policy table and the model are ready; the first data wave is the 2025 YRBS.
  • License GSMA coverage polygons and apply for the MICS and DHS cluster coordinates, then test the adolescent-reported phone-hours items (GSHS 2022–23, MICS7) against local coverage timing. This is the one design that measures exposure on the adolescent herself while using something other than her own choices to explain it.
  • Apply for the HBSC microdata to run the concentration test (are complaints increasingly concentrated among high-pressure students?) and the student-level social-media × pressure interaction, which is the only available test of H3.
  • Stop extending the ACS market event study and the census cognitive item; they have done what they can. Defer the restricted-data route (the Census Bureau research data centers) until a state-survey or ban result justifies it.
