Teen Distress Pilot

Two recent accounts offer competing explanations for the post-2010 rise in adolescent anxiety and depression: Jonathan Haidt's The Anxious Generation attributes the shift to smartphones and social media, while Peter Gray points to intensified schooling and declining autonomy. This pilot report asks how far large-scale public microdata can go toward separating those hypotheses before any restricted or licensed datasets are requested. Every figure and table is computed from public microdata (ACS, ATUS, MICS, PISA, GSHS, IPUMS International) and published survey series.

Part 1 · The rise, and whether public data can measure it

Testing either hypothesis requires first establishing the outcome in public data: verifying that adolescent distress rose after 2010, checking whether the increase reflects a genuine shift rather than survey reporting drift, and evaluating how closely the Census disability screener tracks clinical depression and self-reported sadness. This section combines the American Community Survey with national mental-health surveys (NSDUH, YRBS, NHANES) and county-level state youth surveys.

The ACS cognitive-difficulty item, 2008–2024

The American Community Survey asks of every household member aged 5 and older whether the person has "serious difficulty concentrating, remembering, or making decisions" because of a physical, mental, or emotional condition. The question wording has been unchanged since 2008 (2005–07 used different wording and is excluded). Each annual wave samples roughly 240,000 adolescents aged 12–17. The ACS age series establish two basic facts. First, the pre-pandemic 2010s increase in cognitive difficulty is statistically clear but modest in absolute terms (about half a percentage point on a baseline of 3–5%), and it appears across all age groups under 50—rising fastest among 18–24-year-olds—while falling steadily among adults 65 and older. Second, the post-2020 surge is much steeper and is heavily concentrated among girls and young women. Because the ACS question is a severe disability screener, it will miss mood or anxiety disorders that do not impair daily concentration; the next section evaluates how well it tracks direct measures of adolescent depression.

Does the ACS item measure adolescent distress?

Before using the ACS cognitive-difficulty item as an outcome, we test it against three criteria: whether its national timing matches established adolescent depression and sadness surveys, whether its increase is concentrated among girls, and whether state-level changes in the ACS item move with state-level changes in clinical depression. Because the public NHANES depression screener (PHQ-9) is released only for respondents aged 18 and older (the adolescent file is restricted), NHANES enters as an 18–24 benchmark.

Do states move together?

In cross-section, state levels of the ACS item and NSDUH depression do not correlate, which is unsurprising given that one is a broad disability screener and the other is a diagnostic interview. Over time, however, their within-state changes move together: a state-and-period fixed-effects regression of NSDUH youth depression on the ACS cognitive-difficulty rate yields a slope of 0.14 (SE 0.05, p = 0.01) across 714 state-period observations, and the decade-long changes from 2008–10 to 2017–19 correlate at r = 0.22 (slope 0.37, SE 0.18). Against the YRBS state files (39 states, by sex), the ACS rate co-moves with persistent sadness at a slope of 0.35 (SE 0.18) with state-by-sex and year-by-sex fixed effects, though not with suicidal ideation.

Assessment. The ACS cognitive-difficulty item reproduces the female-led rise and co-moves with depression within states, making it usable for fine-grained geographic tests where no mental-health survey exists. Even so, it captures only about a sixth of the proportional rise in adolescent depression, and its girl-specific change is small relative to metro-level sampling error. For that reason, we pair every U.S. ACS test with state and county youth mental-health surveys.

Geographic variation: is there anything to explain?

For a geographic rollout design to have statistical power, 2010s changes in adolescent distress must vary across local markets by more than sampling noise. Grouping the ACS into metropolitan areas (plus each state's non-metro remainder), the weighted mean change from 2008–10 to 2017–19 among 12–17-year-olds is +0.47 percentage points, the estimated true between-market standard deviation is 0.46 points, and the median market-level sampling error is 0.64 points (Cochran's Q = 218 on 116 df, I² ≈ 0.47). Real cross-market differences exist, but individual metro estimates are noisy and must be pooled in regression models.

States, by sex

Disaggregating state-level changes by sex reveals a major constraint for U.S. geographic designs: while boys' changes vary substantially across states (I² ≈ 0.46), girls' changes are nearly uniform nationwide (I² ≈ 0.05). Because the female series is the component that reflects internalizing distress, there is very little cross-state variation in the ACS for either a phone-rollout or a school-policy design to exploit.

State youth surveys at county level

To measure self-reported adolescent distress below the state level, we compiled county-level school survey tables from six states: Minnesota (1998–2019, 87 counties), Washington (2010–2025, 38 counties), Maine (2009–2023, 16 counties), California (2013–2019, 55 counties, by sex), Pennsylvania (2015–2023, 60 counties), and Illinois (2018–2024, 82 counties for sadness and 75 for suicidal ideation), totaling 34,439 county-grade-year cells with documented question wording and primary sources. Minnesota's pre-2013 sadness question asked about feeling sad over the past 30 days rather than the standard YRBS two-week item and is excluded from the sadness panel.

Unweighted county means across the grade levels each state reports in every survey wave (Washington grades 8, 10, and 12; Maine's 9–12 and Pennsylvania's 6–12 all-grade aggregates; Minnesota grade 9; California grades 7, 9, and 11 for sadness and 9 and 11 for suicidal ideation; Illinois grades 8, 10, and 12 for sadness and 10 and 12 for suicidal ideation). Minnesota's 2022 county tables are behind a web portal CAPTCHA and are not yet included.

The county panels highlight three notable patterns. First, persistent sadness rose across every state from roughly 2013 to a peak in 2021 (for example, from 28% to 41% in Washington, 23% to 37% in Maine, and 35% to 41% in Illinois). Second, Minnesota's 9th-grade suicidal-ideation series—the only county series extending back to the 1990s—fell steadily from 24% in 1998 to 12% in 2013 before turning upward to 15% by 2019. Third, the post-2021 waves show a substantial downturn: Washington's 2025 sadness rate (27%) is below its 2010 baseline, and Illinois's 2024 suicidal-ideation rate (13.5%) is the lowest in its county series.

Part 2 · Phones and social media (H1)

The phone hypothesis predicts that adolescent distress rose earlier in places where smartphones and fast mobile data arrived first, that heavier users experience worse mental health, and that similar patterns hold in developing countries where mobile phones are the primary way young people go online. The tests below move from coarse geographic exposure (metro 4G launch dates and state adoption shares) to individual-level exposure in low- and middle-income countries.

4G rollout across U.S. metros

If the arrival of fast mobile data drove the initial upturn in adolescent distress, teenagers in metropolitan areas that received commercial 4G LTE earliest should have experienced an earlier rise in cognitive difficulty and sadness.

We compiled commercial 4G LTE launch dates from carrier press releases and trade-press archives covering 967 carrier-market launches (Verizon 442, AT&T 243, Sprint 234, T-Mobile 47). Mapped to 2013 CBSA boundaries, these records establish a first-launch date for 98 of the 100 largest metropolitan areas and 279 of the 311 metros identified in the public ACS. Verizon's initial December 2010 rollout covered 36 of these metros, followed by 125 metros in 2011, 96 in 2012, and 14 in 2013. Each market's treatment onset is assigned to the first school year beginning after its initial commercial launch. Remaining coverage gaps (primarily Verizon's smaller 2013 rural market waves and roughly 44 AT&T launch dates outside the top 100 metros) are documented in the methods note.

Assessment. Staggered 4G LTE launch dates across U.S. metropolitan areas do not predict the timing of the rise in the ACS cognitive-difficulty item. For girls, the 95% confidence interval rules out effects larger than +0.33 pp on the dated-metro sample and +0.12 pp when including non-metro controls, compared with a national 2010–2019 increase of roughly 0.5 pp. Three limitations prevent this null result from ruling out H1: commercial LTE launches were compressed into a narrow 2011–2012 window across most major metros; adolescent social-media norms spread across markets through national platforms; and the ACS disability item captures only a fraction of internalizing distress. What the result does establish is that a metro-level rollout design using the ACS cannot detect a smartphone effect. In the next subsection we re-run the same geographic timing test on county-level youth mental-health surveys, and in the reconciliation section we compare our setup with Elaine Guo's British Columbia wireless study, which found a large effect on clinical school designations in rural mountain towns.

Measuring self-reported sadness and suicidal ideation at the county level yields the same conclusion as the ACS metro panel: neither 4G LTE launch timing nor metro-level smartphone adoption predicts where adolescent distress rose fastest. Phone-first countries: MICS Child Functioning Module (68 surveys, 2017–2024)

MICS6 and MICS7 surveys (2017 onward) administer the Washington Group / UNICEF Child Functioning Module for children aged 5–17, asking the mother or primary caregiver how often the child "seems very anxious, nervous or worried" and how often the child seems "very sad or depressed" (daily, weekly, monthly, a few times a year, or never), using identical wording across countries. In the same households, women aged 15–49 (and men in roughly half the surveys) report mobile-phone ownership and how often they used the internet and a mobile phone over the past three months, while the household questionnaire records home internet access. Reading all 68 MICS samples with the module on the IPUMS-MICS server yields 507,478 children with valid responses and weights across 67 surveys (53 of which also include the young-adult internet items).

Prevalence across surveys

One point per survey (ages 10–17, across the 53 surveys that also record young-adult internet use). Cross-survey variation in caregiver-reported anxiety is wide—ranging from under 1% in Laos, Tuvalu, Vietnam, and Turkmenistan to 40–50% in Tunisia, Afghanistan, the Central African Republic, and Chad—and largely reflects national reporting norms, which is why all tests below compare children within the same country and region.

Use and symptoms within countries

Cluster rows regress children's daily/weekly anxiety on the share of 15–24-year-olds in their sampling cluster who use the internet almost daily (or own a mobile phone, or the household internet share), with survey-by-region fixed effects, household wealth quintile, urban/rural status, and age controls, and standard errors clustered by sampling cluster. Own-use rows link 15–17-year-olds to their own individual record in the women's or men's questionnaire (85% of girls matched, 24% of boys) and compare users with non-users within the same sampling cluster.

Girls aged 10–17 living in sampling clusters where more young adults use the internet daily are reported as slightly more anxious (+0.34 pp per 10-percentage-point rise in the cluster share, SE 0.13). However, a similar positive association appears for 5–9-year-old girls (+0.19 pp, SE 0.13), who are too young to use phones themselves, indicating that this reflects characteristics of more connected communities rather than adolescent phone use. Cluster-level mobile-phone ownership is negatively associated with caregiver-reported anxiety across all age and sex groups; household internet access shows a small positive association for 10–17-year-old girls (+0.26 pp per 10 points, SE 0.12, and +0.02 pp at ages 5–9); and caregiver-reported depression shows no positive relationship with any connectivity measure. Comparing 15–17-year-olds who use the internet or a phone daily with non-users in the same sampling cluster—the closest MICS equivalent to individual-level screen exposure—yields point estimates for girls that are near zero or slightly negative: −0.1 pp on anxiety (SE 1.4) and −1.8 pp on depression (SE 1.3) for daily internet use, and −2.2 pp on anxiety (SE 1.2) for daily phone use. For boys, the within-cluster own-phone-use coefficients are positive (+4.4 pp on anxiety and +5.5 pp on depression, SE ~2.0), though these rely on the roughly one-quarter of boys interviewed in the men's sub-sample. The learning and friendship functioning items show no meaningful association in either sex.

DHS-8 self-reports: Nepal 2022

The DHS-8 mental-health module provides a self-reported counterpart to the MICS caregiver reports. In 2022, Nepal administered the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 screeners to a subsample of 7,442 women aged 15–49 alongside questions on internet-use frequency and smartphone ownership (Kenya 2022 asked only whether a doctor had ever diagnosed depression or anxiety). Among Nepali women aged 15–19 (n = 1,379; 4.5% with PHQ-9 ≥ 10, 42% using the internet almost daily), daily internet users are 2.3 pp more likely to screen positive for depression (SE 2.4) and 3.5 pp more likely to screen positive for anxiety (SE 2.9) than non-users in the same sampling cluster (n = 1,293 in within-cluster regressions controlling for wealth, education, and age); the corresponding estimates are also positive at ages 20–24 and turn negative at ages 25–49. Smartphone ownership itself shows no positive association, and in Kenya diagnosed depression among 15–19-year-olds is unrelated to owning a smartphone (−0.1 pp, SE 1.3). Although these single-survey cross-sections are too small to be precise, their pattern matches the self-reported GSHS phone-hours gradient rather than the null results in the MICS caregiver reports: the positive association with screen use appears when adolescents report both their own use and their own symptoms.

Assessment. Across 67 MICS surveys, neither local young-adult connectivity nor a teenage girl's own daily internet or phone use predicts caregiver-reported anxiety or depression within sampling clusters, and the small positive cluster correlation for girls' anxiety is shared by 5–9-year-olds. Asking adolescents directly: mobile-phone hours in the 2022–23 rounds

Five recent GSHS rounds (Ghana 2022, India 2022, Jamaica 2023, Morocco 2023, and Wallis and Futuna 2023) added a question on "hours per day you used a mobile phone in the past 7 days," pairing each student's self-reported phone time with the same student's loneliness and suicidality responses.

Regressions include country fixed effects and age controls, survey weights, and standard errors clustered by school across 7,708 girls and 7,102 boys (including students without a phone). Compared with students using a phone under three hours a day or not at all, girls at three or more hours report loneliness +8.2 pp (SE 1.4), suicidal ideation +10.1 pp (SE 1.5), and suicide attempts +5.6 pp (SE 1.6); the corresponding differences for boys are +5.3, +3.6, and +1.9 pp. Distress rises steadily across higher-use categories: relative to under one hour per day, girls reporting more than eight hours per day have +19.5 pp higher loneliness, +17.4 pp higher suicidal ideation, and +13.7 pp higher suicide attempts. Students without a mobile phone sit a few percentage points above the under-one-hour group rather than below it.

This individual-level dose-response gradient matches the pattern documented in Western surveys: substantial in magnitude, roughly twice as steep for girls as for boys, and increasing monotonically with daily hours above one hour. Because it is a cross-sectional self-report association, however, it cannot distinguish the effect of phone use on distress from reverse causation or shared underlying vulnerabilities. What it does show is that the 2022–23 GSHS rounds (alongside incoming MICS7 adolescent modules) are strong candidates for linking to subnational mobile-coverage rollout maps, since exposure is measured directly on the adolescent rather than inferred from national or adult averages.

Phone-first countries: census microdata (IPUMS International)

We also examined the 2009/2019 Kenya and 2010/2020 Mexico census pairs in IPUMS International, the two best candidates carrying disability items alongside connectivity measures. Part 3 · School pressure (H2)

The school-pressure hypothesis predicts that adolescent distress rose when and where academic demands intensified: during the high-stakes high-school years, in states that introduced test-based accountability reforms, and in countries where students report rising schoolwork pressure. We test this channel using U.S. adolescent time-use diaries, the cross-national HBSC schoolwork-pressure series, and state accountability policy timelines.

Did school demands rise? Time use of 15–17-year-olds (ATUS, 2003–2024)

The American Time Use Survey (ATUS) measures how U.S. 15–17-year-olds allocate their time on school-year weekdays. Between 2003–08 and 2015–19, average weekday homework time rose from roughly 50 minutes to 71 minutes per day (+40%) and remained near 69 minutes through 2023–24. Over the same period, sleep duration did not decline (averaging 8.7–9.1 hours), in-person socializing drifted downward before dropping sharply after 2020, television viewing fell, and time spent playing computer or video games more than doubled. Sample sizes are modest (roughly 100 to 800 teens per pooled period), and because ATUS records only primary activities, background smartphone checking during homework or socializing is not captured.

Part 4 · The interaction (H3): phones in the same hours as high-pressure school

In its sharpest form, the interaction hypothesis (H3) proposes that smartphones harm adolescents primarily when they pervade the school day alongside high-stakes academic pressure—layering constant social comparison and notifications onto the same hours that carry grades and testing. That mechanism implies three testable predictions: academic demands and smartphone use should both have risen after 2010 (as seen in HBSC schoolwork pressure and U.S. weekday homework time); removing phones from the school day should improve well-being, particularly for students experiencing high academic pressure; and screen use outside high-pressure school environments should be less strongly tied to distress.

Plotted on a common timeline, U.S. school phone restrictions and adolescent distress moved in opposite directions over two successive cycles. Between 2009–10 and 2015–16—the years when smartphones replaced feature phones—the share of public schools prohibiting cell-phone use fell from 91% to 66%, while youth depression and sadness rose from their 2011–13 lows. School prohibitions then rebounded to 77% by 2019–20, and a wave of state legislation between 2023 and 2026 has pushed mandatory class-time or bell-to-bell bans into a majority of states; over the same post-2021 window, adolescent sadness fell across every state survey with recent data. Because these are aggregate national series, however, the timing alignment is only suggestive: sadness continued to rise through 2019–2021 even after school prohibitions began recovering, and the post-2021 downturn started before most state laws took effect. Two non-exclusive mechanisms could account for the recent decline:

  • Gradual school re-restriction and broader norm shifts. The recovery in school restrictions after 2016, combined with growing concern among parents, educators, and teenagers themselves, may have curbed the most disruptive in-school phone use even before statutory state mandates arrived. That interpretation is consistent with the PISA 2022 cross-section and Norway's middle-school ban study, though the aggregate U.S. timing is loose.
  • Shifts in platform content. If the 2012–2019 rise was driven primarily by peer social comparison on photo-sharing networks (Instagram, Snapchat), the subsequent shift of teen screen time toward algorithmic short-form video feeds (TikTok, YouTube) could keep total screen hours high while generating less acute social-comparison distress. Because public surveys do not track platform mix over time, our data cannot test this channel directly.

Lags and enforcement

Two practical features of school phone bans also affect how the aggregate timeline and upcoming state evaluations should be interpreted:

  • Effects take a year or two to materialize. Quasi-experimental studies consistently find adjustment lags after a ban is introduced. In the 2026 U.S. Yondr-pouch study (Allcott et al.), student subjective well-being dipped by 0.20 SD in the adoption year before turning positive (+0.16 SD) in year two; in Norway (Abrahamsson 2024), reductions in bullying and mental-health visits grew over three years; and in Florida (Figlio and Özek 2025), student suspensions rose 12% in year one before fading in year two as test-score gains emerged. If school bans require a year of administrative adjustment before improving school climate, the 2015–16 trough in U.S. restrictions would register around 2017–18 and the 2024–25 state mandates around 2026–27. Evaluations of the new U.S. laws will therefore need at least two post-ban waves rather than a single immediate post-year.
  • Stated policy differs from actual enforcement. PISA 2022 measures both principal-reported school bans and student-reported daily smartphone use at school. Across 52 education systems with both items, a median 46% of students in ban schools still reported using a smartphone at school daily (versus 62% in non-ban schools), and in 24 systems a majority of students in ban schools did so. Enforcement in the United States in 2022 was particularly weak: 70.1% of students in U.S. ban schools used a smartphone daily at school compared with 81.7% in non-ban schools (an 11.6-point gap, versus gaps of 30–46 points in Japan, Taiwan, Switzerland, and Brunei). Stated school policies in the pre-2024 U.S. data were thus loosely enforced, and any effects estimated from early state mandates will represent a lower bound on strict bell-to-bell enforcement.

Across existing evaluations, evidence on school phone bans is mixed overall but points to girl-specific benefits when mental health is measured over multiple years. The strongest mental-health evidence comes from Abrahamsson's (2024) staggered difference-in-differences study of more than 400 Norwegian middle schools, which finds that bans reduced girls' general-practitioner and specialist visits for psychological symptoms, reduced bullying for both sexes, and raised girls' GPA, with no mental-health or achievement effect for boys. By contrast, an English cross-sectional study (Goodyear et al. 2025) finds no difference in student well-being between restrictive and permissive schools, and the 2026 U.S. Yondr-pouch study (Allcott et al.) finds an initial year-one dip in subjective well-being (−0.20 SD) followed by a year-two recovery (+0.16 SD) and no change in perceived online bullying. On academic achievement, Beland and Murphy (2016) in England, Beneito and Vicente-Chirivella (2022) in Spain, Figlio and Özek (2025) in Florida, and Lichand et al. The within-country comparisons show three consistent patterns. First, school bans reduce in-school phone use, though compliance is far from complete: daily smartphone use at school is 12.1 percentage points lower in ban schools (from an overall mean of 55.3%, meaning roughly 43% of students in ban schools still use a phone daily), leisure digital use of more than an hour per day at school falls by 3.7 points, and after-school leisure screen use (>3 hours/day) is nearly unchanged (−0.8 pp)—a useful check that ban schools are not simply attended by students who avoid screens outside school. Second, students in ban schools report less classroom distraction by digital devices (−3.5 pp overall; −4.2 pp for girls and −2.8 pp for boys). Third, well-being differences are small, favorable, and concentrated among girls: girls in ban schools are 2.0 pp less likely to feel nervous or anxious without their device, 1.4 pp less likely to report feeling lonely at school (versus +0.3 pp for boys), and score +0.02 to +0.03 SD higher on school belonging and life satisfaction. Because this is a cross-sectional comparison across schools, however, it cannot separate the causal effect of a phone ban from unobserved differences in school leadership or student composition.

Assessment. Across 80 education systems in PISA 2022, school phone prohibitions are associated with lower in-school phone use, less classroom digital distraction, and slightly lower loneliness and device anxiety among girls. While cross-sectional school comparisons cannot rule out selection into bans, the girl-specific pattern aligns with the quasi-experimental evidence from Norway's middle-school bans and underscores the value of evaluating the 2024–26 U.S. state phone bans as post-ban YRBS and state-survey waves become available.

How this sits with Haidt's and Twenge's evidence

The Anxious Generation research pages and collaborative reviews compile a large body of evidence linking smartphones and social media to adolescent distress. Much of the descriptive case comes from Jean Twenge's work with the Monitoring the Future, YRBS, and Millennium Cohort surveys (correlating screen time with depressive symptoms and tracking national trends), paired with natural experiments on broadband and early social-media rollouts. Placing that literature alongside our pilot results clarifies what the public microdata can and cannot confirm: the individual-level association between heavy phone use and distress replicates readily in new settings, but population-level timing tests in public data show no effect, and most of the published causal studies test earlier technologies rather than the post-2010 smartphone transition.

Where the pilot agrees

  • In the 2022–23 GSHS rounds across five countries, girls who use a mobile phone three or more hours a day report loneliness 8 percentage points higher and suicidal ideation 10 points higher than girls who use a phone less, with rates rising steadily at higher hours. That corresponds to an odds ratio of roughly 1.5 to 1.7, similar to the cross-sectional estimates in U.S. and European surveys.
  • In the 2022 Nepal DHS, self-reported PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores among 15–19-year-old women show a positive, though imprecisely estimated, within-cluster association with daily internet use.
  • The national U.S. trends line up across sources: NSDUH youth depression turns upward in 2011–12 and YRBS sadness follows, the increases are consistently larger for girls than for boys, and the ACS cognitive-difficulty rate among adults 65 and older falls over the same decade.
  • In state-level panels, the NTIA share of teen households using smartphones for internet access predicts YRBS girls' sadness (+0.9 pp per 10 percentage points, SE 0.44), though this relationship does not hold at the county level.

Where the pilot finds no effect, and how to interpret the difference

  1. LTE rollout at the metro and county levels. Most of the natural experiments cited in the Anxious Generation literature estimate the effect of first wired broadband access in the 2000s, or of Facebook first arriving on college campuses. By contrast, 4G LTE in 2011–2013 was a speed upgrade layered onto existing 3G smartphones and mobile Facebook; most large U.S. metros received LTE within a two-year window; and if social media operates partly through peer norms and national platforms, teenagers in later-adopting markets were exposed alongside early-adopting ones. Each of these factors attenuates geographic comparisons across U.S. markets. Finding a null effect for LTE timing therefore indicates that U.S. metro rollout dates cannot isolate the 2010s shift, not that smartphones were harmless.

    The closest geographic parallel that does find an effect in the smartphone era is Elaine Guo's British Columbia study. Guo compares teenagers in rural mountain communities that gained high-speed fixed wireless internet (25 Mbps over cellular infrastructure) with teenagers in mountain communities that remained unserved, comparing girls with boys before and after 2014 using administrative school records on severe mental-health designations (which require a clinical diagnosis). Starting in 2014, girls in covered communities experienced a roughly 1 percentage point increase in severe designations relative to boys—about 90% of their baseline rate—with no corresponding shift for boys, moderate designations, or unrelated disabilities. Three features distinguish Guo's setting from our U.S. LTE panel: the treatment is a transition from little or no home broadband to high-speed access in isolated towns rather than a 3G-to-4G upgrade in metropolitan areas; the outcome is a clinical school designation rather than a census disability screener; and the time contrast compares pre- and post-2014 periods against a 2019 coverage map rather than staggered rollout dates (which is also a limitation of that study, since earlier annual coverage maps were unavailable). Taken together, the two analyses suggest that geographic designs can detect a 2010s connectivity effect only where broadband access itself was newly introduced and where the outcome captures clinical internalizing disorders.
  2. Country-level GSHS panels with national internet, mobile-broadband, and 3G/4G series. Regressing country-round survey means on national ITU penetration series is subject to the same aggregation problems that Haidt and coauthors highlight in Vuorre and Przybylski's 72-country Facebook study: national averages pool adult and youth access and move with broader economic trends. Our country-level GSHS regressions should be read with the same caution.
  3. Caregiver reports in 67 MICS surveys. The MICS analysis provides a more substantive null result. Across 507,478 children in low- and middle-income countries, caregiver-reported anxiety and depression among 10–17-year-olds show no meaningful relationship with local young-adult internet use or with a teenage girl's own daily phone or internet use within the same sampling cluster; the small positive cluster correlation for girls' anxiety is mirrored among 5–9-year-olds who do not use phones. Two interpretations remain open: either the self-report correlations in school surveys partly reflect reverse causation and shared reporting style (distressed adolescents both use phones more and report more symptoms), or caregivers frequently miss internalizing symptoms in their children (consistent with clinical evidence that parent–child agreement on internalizing disorders is low). Public cross-sections cannot separate these two explanations, which motivates linking adolescent self-reports to external data on local network rollout.

Two empirical patterns the existing debate has largely overlooked

  • Perceived schoolwork pressure rose after 2010 across roughly 45 HBSC countries, not only in the United States, and psychosomatic complaints rose more in countries where pressure increased more. Although the within-country slope is modest and matches the pre-2010 baseline relationship, the post-2010 rise in perceived academic pressure is a broad cross-national trend that dismissals based on single-country exam reforms do not address.
  • In U.S. state youth surveys with post-2021 waves, adolescent sadness and suicidal ideation have fallen substantially from their 2021 peaks—in Washington, 2025 sadness is below its 2010 rate—without a corresponding drop in daily smartphone or social-media use. Neither a simple screen-dose account nor a simple academic-pressure account explains that downturn without additional mechanisms, such as changes in school phone restrictions or shifts in platform content.
Summary. Prior literature documents a consistent individual-level correlation between heavy screen use and adolescent distress alongside causal effects from earlier broadband and Facebook rollouts, while Guo's British Columbia study extends that causal evidence to rural wireless access and clinical diagnoses after 2014. Our pilot reproduces the individual-level association in developing-country school surveys, but finds that U.S. 4G rollout dates, county adoption rates, and caregiver reports in 67 MICS surveys show no corresponding effect. Resolving whether the self-reported association is causal requires pairing adolescent-reported symptoms and phone use with exogenous local variation—most promisingly, the staggered 2024–26 U.S. school phone bans and subnational mobile-coverage rollouts.

Conclusions and next steps

Combining public microdata across the United States and more than 80 other countries narrows the debate in several ways while leaving the core causal question open.

HypothesisWhere it stands after the pilot
H1 · Phones and social mediaThe cross-sectional individual association is robust and extends to developing countries: teenage girls who use mobile phones heavily report substantially higher loneliness and suicidal ideation in Ghana, India, Jamaica, Morocco, and Nepal just as in Western surveys, with the same sex asymmetry and dose-response gradient. Yet every test based on rollout timing or adult- and cluster-measured exposure is null: 4G launch dates across U.S. metros and counties, metro adoption rates in the county panels, cluster connectivity and own use in 67 MICS caregiver surveys, and national 3G/4G coverage in 34 GSHS countries. Either the causal effect operates through peer norms and national platforms too uniformly for subnational timing designs to detect, or the individual correlation largely reflects reverse causation and shared vulnerabilities. School phone bans in PISA 2022 and Norway provide modest evidence of a girl-specific benefit.
H2 · School pressureThe descriptive premise of Gray's account holds up better than critics often acknowledge: perceived schoolwork pressure rose after 2010 across most HBSC countries, and U.S. weekday homework time rose by roughly 40%. However, perceived pressure behaves like a steady correlate of psychosomatic complaints (with the same within-country slope before and after 2010) rather than the driver of the post-2010 break; U.S. state accountability reforms show no effect; and the ACS age gradient does not peak in the high-stakes high-school years. A key constraint is measurement: public U.S. surveys lack longitudinal school-level pressure items, making H2 harder to test subnationally than H1.
H3 · InteractionState accountability policies interacted with metro 4G launch dates show no positive effect on the ACS item, but the school-day version of the interaction hypothesis—smartphones operating inside high-pressure school hours—is consistent with the school-ban evidence: bans reduce daily in-school phone use and classroom distraction, girls in ban schools report lower loneliness and device anxiety, the loneliness reduction is directionally larger among students with high mathematics anxiety, Norway's middle-school bans reduced girls' psychological consultations, and U.S. adolescent sadness moved inversely to national school phone restrictions.
H0 · MeasurementRuled out as the primary explanation: the post-2010 rise appears simultaneously across diagnostic interviews, self-report school surveys, and the ACS cognitive-difficulty item; the 65+ comparison group trends downward over the same decade; and the increase is consistently concentrated among girls. Differential awareness and diagnosis-seeking likely contribute to the steeper relative rise among affluent and private-school teens, but do not account for the overall trend.

Three empirical findings from the pilot stand out beyond the existing book-length arguments. First, the 2010s rise in adolescent distress occurred almost simultaneously across U.S. states and counties with very different mobile-rollout and school-policy timelines, which explains why coarse geographic designs struggle to separate the competing hypotheses and why national time series can be cited in support of either view. Second, post-2021 waves of the U.S. state youth surveys show a substantial decline in sadness and suicidal ideation—returning to or below 2010 levels in Washington and Illinois—without a matching drop in daily phone or social-media use, coinciding instead with the re-tightening of school phone rules. Any purely dose-based account of screen time or school pressure has to account for that downturn. Third, across developing-country surveys, the link between phone use and distress appears consistently when adolescents report both their own hours and their own symptoms (GSHS, Nepal DHS), but vanishes when symptoms are reported by caregivers (67 MICS surveys). Resolving why self-reports and caregiver reports diverge is central to the next phase of work.
