The Anxious Generation research pages and collaborative reviews compile a large body of evidence linking smartphones and social media to adolescent distress, while Peter Gray and education researchers point to declining childhood autonomy, academic pressure, and the school calendar (Gray, Lancy, and Bjorklund 2023; Hansen, Sabia, and Schaller 2024). Much of the descriptive case for the phone hypothesis comes from Jean Twenge's work with the Monitoring the Future, YRBS, and Millennium Cohort surveys (correlating screen time with depressive symptoms and tracking national trends), paired with natural experiments on broadband, early social-media rollouts, and pre-2011 carrier exclusivity. Placing both literatures alongside our pilot results clarifies what public microdata can and cannot confirm: the individual-level association between heavy phone use and distress replicates readily in new settings, both camps converge on a long-run decline in teens' in-person socializing, and the sharpest quasi-experimental effects come from initial broadband or iPhone access and the school calendar rather than 2011–13 4G speed upgrades.

Where the pilot agrees

  • In the 2022–23 GSHS rounds across five countries, girls who use a mobile phone three or more hours a day report loneliness 8 percentage points higher and suicidal ideation 10 points higher than girls who use a phone less, with rates rising steadily at higher hours. That corresponds to an odds ratio of roughly 1.5 to 1.7, similar to the cross-sectional estimates in U.S. and European surveys.
  • In the 2022 Nepal DHS, self-reported PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores among 15–19-year-old women show a positive, though imprecisely estimated, within-cluster association with daily internet use.
  • The national U.S. trends line up across sources: NSDUH youth depression turns upward in 2011–12 and YRBS sadness follows, the increases are consistently larger for girls than for boys, and the ACS cognitive-difficulty rate among adults 65 and older falls over the same decade. In ATUS time diaries, weekday homework time rose by roughly 40% while teens' in-person socializing fell—consistent with both Gray's emphasis on rising academic structure and Haidt's account of declining face-to-face peer time.
  • In state-level panels, the NTIA share of teen households using smartphones for internet access predicts YRBS girls' sadness (+0.9 pp per 10 percentage points, SE 0.44). That aligns directionally with Churchill and Johnson (2026), who link FCC state broadband expansion to the 2009–2019 YRBS and find a 9–17% increase in adolescent suicidal ideation per standard deviation of broadband access (concentrated among girls alongside cyberbullying and body dissatisfaction), though in our county-level youth survey panels the adoption relationship does not hold within states.

Where the pilot finds no effect, and how to interpret the difference

  1. LTE rollout at the metro and county levels. Most of the natural experiments cited in the Anxious Generation literature estimate the effect of first high-speed broadband access (Donati et al. 2025; Arenas-Arroyo et al. 2025), of Facebook first arriving on college campuses, or of early U.S. smartphone exclusivity. On that last point, Myers and Hooper (2026) show that during AT&T's exclusive U.S. carrier monopoly on the iPhone from June 2007 to February 2011, pre-existing county-level AT&T 3G coverage strongly predicted early iPhone adoption, crowding out teens' in-person socializing and reducing 15–19 birth rates by 4.5–8.0%. By the time commercial 4G LTE rolled out across U.S. metros in 2011–2013—and after Verizon began carrying the iPhone in February 2011—3G smartphones and mobile social media were already spreading nationwide, and most large metros received LTE within a narrow two-year window. Finding a null effect for 4G LTE launch dates therefore shows that 2011–13 metro speed upgrades are too late and compressed to isolate the U.S. smartphone transition, whereas pre-2011 AT&T 3G coverage and county broadband availability offer sharper variation.

    The closest post-2010 geographic parallel that does find a mental-health effect is Elaine Guo's British Columbia study (alongside Arenas-Arroyo et al.'s Spanish fiber-to-the-home study using hospital records). Guo compares teenagers in rural mountain communities that gained high-speed fixed wireless internet (25 Mbps over cellular infrastructure) with teenagers in mountain communities that remained unserved, comparing girls with boys before and after 2014 using administrative school records on severe mental-health designations (which require a clinical diagnosis). Starting in 2014, girls in covered communities experienced a roughly 1 percentage point increase in severe designations relative to boys—about 90% of their baseline rate—with no corresponding shift for boys, moderate designations, or unrelated disabilities. Three features distinguish Guo's setting from our U.S. LTE panel: the treatment is a transition from little or no home broadband to high-speed access in isolated towns rather than a 3G-to-4G upgrade in metropolitan areas; the outcome is a clinical school designation rather than a census disability screener; and the time contrast compares pre- and post-2014 periods against a 2019 coverage map rather than staggered rollout dates (which is also a limitation of that study, since earlier annual coverage maps were unavailable). Taken together, these studies suggest that geographic designs detect a 2010s connectivity effect when they capture initial high-speed access or pre-2011 carrier exclusivity and measure clinical or self-reported internalizing outcomes.
  2. Country-level GSHS panels with national internet, mobile-broadband, and 3G/4G series. Regressing country-round survey means on national ITU penetration series is subject to the same aggregation problems that Haidt and coauthors highlight in Vuorre and Przybylski's 72-country Facebook study: national averages pool adult and youth access and move with broader economic trends. Our country-level GSHS regressions should be read with the same caution.
  3. Caregiver reports in 67 MICS surveys. The MICS analysis provides a more substantive null result. Across 507,478 children in low- and middle-income countries, caregiver-reported anxiety and depression among 10–17-year-olds show no meaningful relationship with local young-adult internet use or with a teenage girl's own daily phone or internet use within the same sampling cluster; the small positive cluster correlation for girls' anxiety is mirrored among 5–9-year-olds who do not use phones. Two interpretations remain open: either the self-report correlations in school surveys partly reflect reverse causation and shared reporting style (distressed adolescents both use phones more and report more symptoms), or caregivers frequently miss internalizing symptoms in their children (consistent with clinical evidence that parent–child agreement on internalizing disorders is low). Public cross-sections in MICS alone cannot separate these two explanations, but they can be tested directly in the U.S. by linking parent and teen reports in NHIS-Teen and internationally by expanding the DHS-8 self-report mental-health panel.

Two empirical patterns the existing debate has largely overlooked

  • Perceived schoolwork pressure rose after 2010 across roughly 45 HBSC countries, not only in the United States, and psychosomatic complaints rose more in countries where pressure increased more. Although the within-country slope is modest and matches the pre-2010 baseline relationship, the post-2010 rise in perceived academic pressure is a broad cross-national trend that dismissals based on single-country exam reforms do not address. Combined with Hansen, Sabia, and Schaller's (2024) finding that U.S. youth suicides drop sharply every summer, fell by 18–25% during the spring 2020 pandemic school closures despite surging screen time, and rebounded when in-person schooling resumed, the evidence points toward an interaction (H3): digital peer comparison and notifications are most harmful when layered onto the social and academic stressors of the school year.
  • In U.S. state youth surveys with post-2021 waves, adolescent sadness and suicidal ideation have fallen substantially from their 2021 peaks—in Washington, 2025 sadness is below its 2010 rate—without a corresponding drop in daily smartphone or social-media use. Neither a simple screen-dose account nor a simple academic-pressure account explains that downturn without additional mechanisms, such as the re-tightening of school phone restrictions or shifts in platform content.
Summary. Prior literature documents a consistent individual-level correlation between heavy screen use and adolescent distress alongside causal effects from initial broadband rollouts (Donati et al. 2025; Arenas-Arroyo et al. 2025; Churchill and Johnson 2026; Guo 2023), early iPhone carrier exclusivity (Myers and Hooper 2026), and the school calendar (Hansen, Sabia, and Schaller 2024). Our pilot reproduces the individual-level association in developing-country school surveys, but finds that 2011–13 U.S. 4G launch dates, county smartphone-adoption rates, and caregiver reports in 67 MICS surveys show no corresponding effect. Resolving where the causal effect lies requires pairing self-reported and clinical outcomes with sharper local variation—including the 2024–26 U.S. school phone bans, pre-2011 carrier coverage, school-year seasonality, and linked parent–teen surveys.
